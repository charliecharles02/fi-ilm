My view on cryptocurrencies

A currency is a unit of exchange that facilitates the trade of goods and services. For something to be considered a currency, it must have 5 properties:

  • 1/ Scarcity: unit supply must be limited
  • 2/ Fungibility: units must be identical and interchangeable
  • 3/ Divisibility: units can be broken into smaller fractions to enable transactions
  • 4/ Durability: units must withstand use and transfer over time
  • 5/ Acceptance: unit's value must be recognized and accepted by a community

Until recently, currencies have respected these properties thanks to a superior system that guaranteed them, most commonly, the state. States stand above citizens, set the rules of social life, and ensure their currency is: scarce (they create it through the mint and control its supply), technically sound in terms of fungibility, divisibility and durability, and accepted by citizens and institutions within their territory.

This was the situation until cryptocurrencies were created. A cryptocurrency is a digital unit created by the same users who guarantee its security. It is a self-sustaining system: the system gives you an amount of cryptocurrency, and in exchange you verify that other users are not falsifying transactions or creating counterfeit coins. This system is called blockchain and, for the first time in history, has made it possible to remove currencies from the control of any superior authority (e.g., the state). The system relies entirely on its users.

Cryptocurrencies therefore have all the properties of a normal currency, with one addition: privacy. Having no central controlling authority means zero oversight of transactions and complete anonymity for users.

However, these currencies have two significant problems. First, states and other central authorities that manage traditional currencies have no interest in letting crypto become the standard. It would mean giving up control and oversight over their citizens. These authorities are not powerless: they have executive force (armies, police, prisons) and significant influence over core businesses (e.g., banks). This creates one of the main flaws of cryptocurrencies: you can barely use it to buy anything. Second, because there is no central authority providing security, many financial instruments are simply impossible to offer in crypto. The clearest example is loans. A bank lends you money because it has your identity and knows that, if you default, the state's legal and executive apparatus will intervene to protect them. That guarantee does not exist with crypto. Total privacy limits trust, and this limits what the system can do.

These two main flaws make crypto extremely impractical for common people and everyday uses. So, who are the people who, despite its evident impracticality, would still buy crypto? There are three groups.

  • 1/ Criminals. They love crypto. It offers the anonymity of cash but is far more practical (no need for a bank or somewhere to hide it, instant digital transfers that leave no trace). Drug dealers, human traffickers, and others in equally terrible businesses use it for exactly these reasons. In this group I also include some honest citizens oppressed by dictatorships, who need to hold money hidden from their government and move it freely across borders. Of the three groups, this is the only one that uses crypto out of genuine necessity.
  • 2/ Dreamers. People who save in crypto — not invest, save. They believe crypto will be the money of the future and are positioning themselves accordingly, treating it like gold. The problem is they haven't accounted for states, which have no intention of replacing their own currencies. These people often imagine utopian worlds without borders or governments, running on decentralized digital money. I will let you think for yourself about the likelihood of this scenario.
  • 3/ Speculators. They buy crypto expecting the price to rise and to sell for a profit. They often invest without a strong rational basis, following trends and hype. The truth is that the only things that can rationally drive the value of these currencies upward are an increase in illicit transactions or an expansion of populations living under oppressive regimes. Everything else is just noise.

My conclusion is simple: don't buy crypto unless you belong to one of these three groups. And if you do: if you're in the first, I'd suggest reconsidering your life choices if you still can; if you're in the second, try to honestly assess how realistic your vision is and talk to people who will push back on it; if you're in the third, keep speculating only if you have genuinely solid information about macroeconomic trends in the criminal world and in countries under authoritarian rule.
